The forex market is open around the clock from Sunday evening to Friday evening, but it is not equally active around the clock. EUR/USD can move 30 pips in an hour during the London morning and 5 pips in an hour late in the Asian session. The same setup behaves differently in each.

This guide gives the session hours in UTC for both winter and summer, explains which hours carry the most volume, and shows how to check which hours work for *your* trading, which is the question that actually matters.

## The four sessions

Forex has no central exchange, so the "sessions" are the business hours of the big trading centres. Most traders use Sydney, Tokyo, London and New York. The times below are the usual conventions, roughly 8:00 to 17:00 local time in each city (Sydney a little earlier):

| Session | Local time | UTC, northern winter | UTC, northern summer |
|---|---|---|---|
| Sydney | 07:00–16:00 | 20:00–05:00 | 21:00–06:00 |
| Tokyo | 09:00–18:00 | 00:00–09:00 | 00:00–09:00 |
| London | 08:00–17:00 | 08:00–17:00 | 07:00–16:00 |
| New York | 08:00–17:00 | 13:00–22:00 | 12:00–21:00 |

"Winter" and "summer" here mean the clocks in Europe and North America. Sydney's daylight saving runs the other way round (October to April), and Tokyo does not change its clocks at all.

> **Watch the daylight saving gap.** The US moves its clocks on the second Sunday of March and the first Sunday of November; the UK on the last Sundays of March and October. For two to three weeks in spring and about a week in autumn, New York opens one hour earlier than usual compared with London. If you trade the New York open by a fixed UTC time, check the calendar in those weeks.

The week starts when Sydney opens, Sunday evening in Europe, and ends with the New York close on Friday at 17:00 New York time.

## Where the volume is

The London session is the heaviest. In the BIS Triennial Survey for April 2025, global FX trading averaged **$9.6 trillion a day**, and dealers in the **United Kingdom handled about 38%** of it. Four centres (the UK, the US, Singapore and Hong Kong) together accounted for about 75%.

In practice that gives three different kinds of hours:

- **The Asian session** (Tokyo, Sydney): usually the quietest for EUR and GBP pairs. Ranges are smaller, and many pairs drift sideways. Yen, Australian and New Zealand dollar pairs are more active.
- **The London session**: volume picks up fast at the open, and many daily highs and lows are set in the first hours. Most European economic data comes out in this window.
- **The London–New York overlap**: 13:00–17:00 UTC in winter, 12:00–16:00 UTC in summer. Two of the biggest centres are open at the same time, the main US data is released at 08:30 New York time, and the London 4 pm fix sits at the end of it. For the major pairs these are usually the most liquid hours of the day.

After London closes, activity fades through the New York afternoon until the daily rollover at 17:00 New York time, when spreads widen for a few minutes as liquidity providers roll positions.

## Which session suits which pairs

| Pair group | Most active |
|---|---|
| EUR/USD, GBP/USD, USD/CHF | London and the London–New York overlap |
| USD/JPY | Tokyo morning, then London and New York |
| AUD/USD, NZD/USD | Sydney–Tokyo, plus Australian and NZ data releases |
| EUR/GBP, EUR/CHF | London |
| USD/CAD | New York, especially around Canadian data at 08:30 New York time |
| Gold (XAU/USD) | London and New York, around US data |

This is a starting point, not a rule. Volatility changes with news, with the season and with the market regime.

## More movement is not automatically better

A busy session gives you bigger moves, tighter spreads and cleaner fills. It also gives you faster reversals, news spikes and stops hit by noise. A quiet session is cheaper to sit through but often too slow for a breakout strategy.

What matters is the match between your strategy and the hours:

- **Breakout and momentum setups** usually need volume: the London open or the overlap.
- **Range and mean-reversion setups** often work better in quieter hours, when price respects levels instead of running through them.
- **News-driven moves** cluster around releases; see our guide on [trading around news with an economic calendar](/blog/trading-the-news-economic-calendar).

## Find your own best hours

Session tables describe the market. Your journal describes you. The two often disagree: plenty of traders find that their best hours are not the "best" hours in the textbook, because they are tired at the New York open, impatient in the Asian session, or simply better at reading one kind of market.

You need two numbers per hour (or per session) from your own trades:

1. **Net P&L and [expectancy](/glossary/expectancy)**: do you make money in that hour on average?
2. **Number of trades**: is the result based on enough trades to mean anything? A good hour with 6 trades is a hint, not a fact; look for 30 or more.

Then do the same for the day of the week. Common findings: a losing first hour after the open, a bad Friday afternoon, or a strong session that gets wiped out by trading on into the next one.

When you find an hour that consistently loses, the simplest fix is also the most effective: stop trading it. Write it into your plan as a rule ("no new trades after 16:00 UTC") and check whether your results improve over the next 50 trades.

## Key takeaways

- The four sessions are Sydney, Tokyo, London and New York. Their UTC times shift by an hour with daylight saving; check both columns.
- London carries the most volume (about 38% of global FX), and the London–New York overlap is usually the most liquid window for the majors.
- Match the session to the strategy: breakouts like volume, ranges often like quiet hours.
- Your own results by hour and weekday matter more than any table. Drop the hours that consistently lose.
