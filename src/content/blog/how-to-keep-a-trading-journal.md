Most traders who keep a journal still repeat the same mistakes. The problem is rarely the journal itself. It is what goes into it and what happens to it afterwards. A list of entries and exits is a log; a journal answers questions such as *which setup actually pays*, *what happens after my third trade of the day* and *how much do I lose when I move my stop*.

This guide covers what to record, a template you can copy into a spreadsheet, and the review routine that makes the data useful.

## Why a trading journal works

Memory is a poor record keeper. After a losing week most traders remember the one painful trade and forget the five small rule breaks that came before it. A journal replaces that impression with numbers:

- **It shows your real edge.** Your [win rate](/glossary/win-rate), [profit factor](/glossary/profit-factor) and [expectancy](/glossary/expectancy) per setup tell you what to keep and what to drop.
- **It exposes behaviour.** Oversized trades after a loss, trading outside your session or skipping the stop show up as patterns, not anecdotes.
- **It protects funded accounts.** If you trade a prop firm challenge, the journal is where you see how close your losing streaks come to the daily loss limit.

## What to record for every trade

Record the same fields every time. Missing fields are the main reason journals become useless after a month. Split them into what you know before the trade, what the market gives you, and what you think afterwards.

| When | Field | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| Before | Date and session | Shows when you trade best (London, New York, Asia) |
| Before | Symbol and direction | Some traders are profitable on one side only |
| Before | Setup or strategy name | The single most useful filter in your review |
| Before | Entry, stop loss, take profit | Defines your risk (1R) and planned [risk-reward ratio](/glossary/risk-reward-ratio) |
| Before | Position size and risk in % | Reveals inconsistent sizing |
| Before | Reason for the trade (one line) | Forces you to have one |
| After | Exit price and time | Basis for the result |
| After | Net P&L after commission and swap | Gross numbers flatter you |
| After | Result in R ([R-multiple](/glossary/r-multiple)) | Makes trades of different sizes comparable |
| After | Followed the plan? (yes/no) | Separates bad luck from bad decisions |
| After | Emotion before entry | FOMO, revenge, boredom, calm |
| After | Mistake tag | Moved stop, early exit, no stop, oversized |
| After | Chart screenshot | You will not remember the context in a month |

You do not need more than this. A journal with 25 columns gets abandoned; one with 13 consistently filled columns gets reviewed.

## A trading journal template you can copy

If you start in a spreadsheet, use these columns in this order:

```
Date | Session | Symbol | Long/Short | Setup | Entry | Stop | Target | Size | Risk % |
Exit | Net P&L | R | Followed plan | Emotion | Mistake | Screenshot link | Notes
```

Two formulas make the sheet far more useful:

- **R** = Net P&L ÷ (|Entry − Stop| × Size × contract value). A loss of exactly −1R means the stop worked as planned; anything below −1R deserves a note.
- **Expectancy** = the average of the R column. Above zero over 50 or more trades is an edge; below zero is a strategy to fix or drop.

## Step by step: the journaling routine

### 1. Before the session (5 minutes)

Write down the markets you will trade, the economic news that can move them, your maximum number of trades and your daily loss limit. These are the rules your journal will check you against later.

### 2. Before each trade (30 seconds)

Fill in the setup, entry, stop, target, size and one line on why. If you cannot write the reason in one line, that is usually a trade you should not take.

### 3. After each trade (1 minute)

Add the exit, the net result, the R-multiple, whether you followed the plan, the emotion and a screenshot. Do it immediately: an hour later your notes are already rationalised.

### 4. The weekly review (30 minutes)

This is where the journal pays off. Every week, answer the same questions:

1. Which setup had the highest expectancy, and which had a negative one?
2. What was my result on trades where I followed the plan versus where I did not?
3. On which day of the week and in which session did I lose the most?
4. What did my trades after a loss look like (size, result)?
5. Which mistake tag appeared most often, and what did it cost?

Pick **one** change for next week from the answers. One rule you keep beats five you forget.

## Common journaling mistakes

- **Logging only losing trades.** Your winners show what to do more of.
- **Using gross P&L.** Commission and swap can turn a profitable-looking strategy into a losing one. Always use net results.
- **Judging after 10 trades.** A few trades say almost nothing; look for patterns over 30 to 50 trades per setup.
- **Changing the template every week.** Consistency is what makes comparisons possible.
- **Never reviewing.** A journal that is written but not read is a diary.
- **Writing emotions in hindsight.** Record the emotion before the entry, not the one you felt after the result.

## Key takeaways

- Record the same 10 to 15 fields for every trade, including setup, R-multiple, plan adherence and emotion.
- Use net results after costs and measure trades in R.
- Review weekly with fixed questions and change one thing at a time.
- Judge setups on 30 to 50 trades, not on last week.
