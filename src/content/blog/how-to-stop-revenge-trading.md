You take a clean setup, follow the plan and get stopped out. Fine, that happens. Then the market moves exactly where you expected, without you. Ten minutes later you are in again: no setup, bigger size, stop "a bit wider this time". It loses too. By the end of the session one normal loss has turned into the worst day of the month.

That is revenge trading: trading to win back a loss instead of trading your plan. Almost every trader does it at some point. This guide covers why it happens, how to spot it in your own trades, and rules that stop it before it starts.

## Why a loss makes you trade worse

A loss is not just money. It feels like being wrong, and being wrong feels like something to fix right now. Research on decision-making backs this up: in Kahneman and Tversky's work on prospect theory, people felt a loss roughly twice as strongly as a gain of the same size. After a loss, getting back to zero feels more urgent than following the plan.

Three things then happen together:

- **Speed goes up.** You stop waiting for your setup because waiting feels like accepting the loss.
- **Size goes up.** A bigger position promises to win it back in one trade.
- **Standards go down.** Stops get wider or disappear, and "close enough" becomes a setup.

Each one alone is a small rule break. Together they are how a −1R day becomes a −6R day.

## The math of doubling up

The most common revenge pattern is increasing the size after each loss to "make it back". Here is what that does with a 1% starting risk:

| Trade | Risk on this trade | Total lost if it fails |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | 1% | 1% |
| 2 (doubled) | 2% | 3% |
| 3 (doubled) | 4% | 7% |
| 4 (doubled) | 8% | 15% |

Three losses in a row, which is an ordinary sequence for almost any strategy, and the account is down 7% in one session. On a prop firm challenge with a 5% daily loss limit, the account is already gone on the third trade. Doubling up does not need a rare disaster to fail. It only needs a normal losing streak.

## Warning signs in your own trades

Revenge trading is easy to see in hindsight and hard to see in the moment. Your journal shows it clearly if you look for these patterns:

- **Trades opened within minutes of a loss**, especially outside your usual session.
- **Position size above your normal size right after a loss.**
- **More trades on red days than on green days.**
- **Losses bigger than −1R**, which means the stop was moved, widened or missing.
- **Results that get worse with each trade of the day**: the third or fourth trade is where the losses start.
- **Notes like "should have", "market owes me" or no note at all** on the trades after a loss.

If two or more of these show up regularly, revenge trading is costing you money, and you can put a number on how much.

## Seven rules that stop the spiral

Willpower in the moment is unreliable. Rules decided before the session are not. These work best written down and checked by something other than your mood.

### 1. Set a daily loss stop well inside any firm limit

Pick a number that ends the day, for example −2R or two losses in a row. When you hit it, you are done until tomorrow. This one rule caps the damage of every bad day, including the ones where you don't notice you are tilting.

### 2. Cap the number of trades per day

Most strategies have a natural number of setups per session. If yours gives two or three, a cap of three trades stops the "one more" sequence that revenge trading lives on.

### 3. Fix your risk per trade, and never raise it after a loss

Same risk on every trade, calculated from the stop with a [position size calculator](/tools/position-size-calculator). If you change size at all, change it down after losses, never up.

### 4. Use a cooldown after every loss

Fifteen minutes away from the screen after a stop-out. Write one line in your journal about the trade before you look at the chart again. The urge to re-enter usually fades faster than you expect.

### 5. Re-check the setup with a short checklist

Before each entry, answer three questions: Is this a setup from my plan? Is the stop where the idea is wrong? Is the size the same as always? A "no" means no trade.

### 6. Record the emotion before the entry

Tag each trade with how you felt before you clicked: calm, FOMO, revenge, bored. After 50 trades, compare the results by tag. Seeing the cost of your "revenge" trades in money is far more convincing than any rule.

### 7. Review the bad days, not just the bad trades

Once a week, look at your worst day and read the trades in order. Where did the plan stop being followed? Was it after the first loss, the second, the third? That point is where your daily rule should be.

## Key takeaways

- Revenge trading is trading to win back a loss: faster, bigger and with lower standards.
- Doubling up after losses turns three ordinary losses into a 7% day.
- Find it in your journal: quick re-entries, bigger size after losses, losses beyond −1R.
- Decide the rules before the session: a daily loss stop, a trade cap, fixed risk and a cooldown.
